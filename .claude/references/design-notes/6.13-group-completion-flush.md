# §6.13 — design notes (group completion flush)

> Full rationale, history, measurements and rot guards behind the condensed rule §6.13 in `.claude/rules/`. Read on demand when changing this area. Verbatim from the pre-split `performance.md` (2026-11-25).

**§6.13** **Dispatcher completions are flushed per worker GROUP, in one set-based statement.** Two things made the batched completion batch very little. **The buffer was per worker**, and each worker flushed its own whenever it found the channel empty for a moment, so at ten workers a flush held about two jobs: a transaction for every second job. `WarpDispatcherHost` now builds ONE `CompletionBatch` per group and every worker of the group adds to it. It is flushed when it is full (`CompletionBatchSize`), when its oldest entry has waited `CompletionFlushInterval` (a timer started by the first completion of each buffer, so it fires even when every worker is inside a long handler), and the moment the group has nothing left claimed (`DispatcherWorkerAvailability.Outstanding == 0`), which is what keeps a lone job from waiting for the timer. Do not put back a flush on "this worker is idle". **That idle trigger is decided from the count `DispatcherWorkerAvailability.Release()` returns, and marks the current buffer due like a timer does** (`CompletionFlusher.SlotReleased(remaining)`): the dispatcher wakes on the freed slot (§6.19) and can claim the next job before the worker reports, so a live read of `Outstanding` saw a busy group and the completion waited for the timer (`CompletionBatchTests.SlotReleased_GroupIdleButTheNextJobClaimedBeforeTheWorkerSaysSo_WritesTheCompletion`, both databases, fails on the live read). The due mark keeps the highest generation, so a late timer for an older buffer cannot overwrite it. **A timer flushes only the buffer it was started for** (`CompletionBatch` numbers each buffer; `Holds(generation)`), never whatever is buffered when it fires: a buffer flushed early (full, or the group idle) left its timer running, and when that timer flushed the next, moments-old buffer, batches fell from ~15 jobs to ~9 and throughput 9-20% on CI. Nor by `IsTimeElapsed`, whose clock is not the timer's: a timer firing a hair early then stranded its buffer behind a long handler. **The writes are done by one `CompletionFlusher` per group, never by a worker**: workers add and go back to their channel, the flusher loop is woken by whoever sees a trigger and re-checks the triggers before writing, so the group has one flush in flight and the next buffer fills behind it. A worker that wrote the flush itself could not run its next job for that round trip (`BatchedCompletionIntegrationTests.GivenACompletionWriteThatHangs_WhenTheWorkerHasAnotherJob_ThenItRunsIt` fails on that shape). The host stops the flushers after every worker has stopped; the flusher's final flush writes what is left, on a budget of its own (`FinalFlushBudgetSeconds`, 5 s): the host's shutdown token has often fired by then, and cancelling on it dropped every completion still buffered (the shutdown test failed intermittently on it). `StopAsync` waits for the loop only until its token fires, then cancels a hung write (its jobs stay Processing for `StaleJobRecovery`, as after a crash). A completion added after the stop (a handler that outlived it) is written inline, since nothing would write the buffer any more. A flush writes at most `4 × CompletionBatchSize` jobs per statement: completions keep arriving during a write, so a buffer can outgrow its size, and one statement for all of it was unbounded. **Backpressure (#348):** at the same bound (`CompletionBatch.BackpressureThreshold` = 4 × `CompletionBatchSize` unwritten, never below `MinBackpressureThreshold` = 200: at batch size 1 a threshold of 4 was hit under ordinary load and cost +12.8% statements/job and −25% jobs/s on CI), `CompletionFlusher.AddAsync` makes the adding worker wait for the write in flight to end before it takes its next job (it still holds its slot, so the group stops claiming); below it, the check is one comparison. The wait ends on the worker's stopping token (the completion stays buffered for the final flush), or a hung database would hang shutdown. Guards: `CompletionBatchTests.AddAsync_GroupHoldsTheBackpressureThreshold_*`, `AddAsync_WaitingOnAHungWrite_ReturnsWhenTheWorkerStops`. A transient `DbException` (command timeout, dropped connection) gets the deadlock retry and then the split, like a `DbUpdateException`; it used to escape and abandon every drained job. **Every completion is guarded by its run** (`JobRunOwner`, carried on `PendingCompletion`, recorded by the worker from the claimed row before it changes it): a row is written only while it is still `Processing` AND either stamped by the run's worker (a renewal ran) or exactly as the run's claim left it (`CurrentWorkerId` = the claimer AND `LastKeepAlive` = the claim's). The keep-alive is what tells two claims by the same group apart. Without it, a job `StaleJobRecovery` took back while its handler still ran (a long pause, lost renewals) and another claim took was finished by the OLD run: `Completed` over the new run's row. A skipped completion counts nothing and writes no log rows, like a deleted row. **Per-run token (#347):** every claim also stamps a fresh `Job.RunId` (`gen_random_uuid()` / `NEWID()`, both claim forms, both providers) and every exit from `Processing` clears it (completion statements, both single-worker arms, `StaleJobRecovery`, the dispatcher's unclaim). The completion, the renewal and `RenewClaim` add `RunId` equality (null-safe) on top of the worker/keep-alive pair — the run id is what tells a re-claim by the SAME worker apart; the pair stays for rolling deploys, where an older binary can take a job back without clearing the run id. Single-worker finalization is guarded by `RunId` as an EF **concurrency token on `WarpServerContext` only** (no extra statement; **never on the application's `TContext` model** — there it applied to every EF update of a job and two concurrent `BulkRequeueJobs` deadlocked on SQL Server; `RunIdConcurrencyTokenTests` pins it off; a re-finalization after a failed COMMIT restores the claim's run id as the original value, or the retry is rejected and the job stays `Processing`; it also re-adds the attempt's diagnostic rows — the deferred "Processing" row, handler log lines and progress rows, which EF had accepted as saved while the database rolled them back: `FailedFinalizationCounterTestsBase.CompletionCommitFails_ReFinalizedAsFailed_KeepsTheProcessingRowAndTheHandlersLogLines`): a conflict is the row taken back or deleted, and `CommitFinalizationAsync` discards the attempt (logged, nothing counted, nothing announced) instead of re-finalizing it as a handler failure. A new site that moves a job out of `Processing` must clear `RunId`. Guards: `JobRunOwnershipTests` (same-worker re-claim, fresh id per claim, cleared on completion, the EF token), `WorkerExecutionIntegrationTests.GivenTheJobIsClaimedAgainWhileItsHandlerRuns_*` (both modes). Rot guard: `JobRunOwnershipTests` (both providers; fails without the predicate). **The flush wrote one UPDATE and one INSERT per row** through the change tracker. It is now `IWarpSqlQueries.CompleteJobsAsync`: PostgreSQL unnests one array per column in a single statement (a data-modifying CTE updates the jobs, inserts their log rows and returns the ids it wrote); SQL Server reads two JSON documents with `OPENJSON` in one batch. The log rows are inserted only for jobs the update found, so a job whose row was deleted while it ran is skipped with its logs, counts nothing, and cannot fail the batch on the foreign key. **On SQL Server `INNER LOOP JOIN` with the JSON rows outermost and `ROWLOCK` are load-bearing**: without them the join scanned the job table, locked other workers' rows and concurrent flushes deadlocked (the batched-completion tests timed out on it). **The log-row insert pins the same order** (`FROM OPENJSON(@logs) ... INNER LOOP JOIN @done`): written as `WHERE l.j IN (SELECT id FROM @done)` the optimizer could put `@done` outermost and re-parse the whole log document per completed job, which CI cached in some runs once one flusher per group wrote ~60 jobs a flush: ~85,000 pages per flush, SQL Server dispatcher mode ~540 -> ~120 jobs/s, bisected to the per-group flusher (it made flushes large). Pinned, two CI runs of the same benchmark gave 884 and 1,232 jobs/s against 404 and 516 for main (`OPTION (RECOMPILE)` was as fast but recompiles every flush; the hint keeps one plan). Rot guard: `CompletionPlanStabilityTests` (replays the real batch under `STATISTICS XML` and asserts `OPENJSON` is the outer input; fails on the `IN` form). The columns written are the nine finalization assigns (rule 6.10's list, `RunId = NULL` included, which now lives in the two provider statements, not in `MarkFinalizedColumns`); a new one must be added to BOTH statements, and `WorkerPathLockstepTests` is what catches a miss, since single-worker mode still finalizes through EF. Only an `ErrorOccurrence` row (§8.29) is still saved by EF, in the same transaction. `SaveChanges` inside the worker's explicit finalizing transactions runs with `AutoSavepointsEnabled = false`: a failed save abandons the transaction, so the SAVEPOINT / RELEASE pair EF adds was pure overhead (two statements per job in single-worker mode). Measured on the load lab, dispatcher mode, 10 workers, 2,000 jobs, PostgreSQL, publish included: 7.09 statements, 0.98 transactions and 0.293 ms of database time per job before; 2.87, 0.48 and 0.153 after. The set-based statement ALONE, with per-worker buffers, cut statements to 4.61 but RAISED database time to 0.350 ms per job, because a two-job flush pays for a larger statement and gets nothing back: the two changes belong together. Rot guards: `CompletionBatchTests` (the real statement on both providers, a missing row, an injected statement failure isolated by the split), `BatchedCompletionIntegrationTests` (size, time and idle triggers) and `WorkerPathLockstepTests`.
